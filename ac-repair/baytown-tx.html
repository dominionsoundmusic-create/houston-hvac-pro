Coastal air and industrial air, on the same coil

Baytown sits between Galveston Bay and one of the largest petrochemical complexes in the country, and your outdoor unit stands in that air twenty-four hours a day.

Condenser coils here corrode faster than inland. Salt-laden air from the bay attacks aluminium fins and the copper tubing behind them, and industrial air adds its own chemistry. The result is a coil that loses the ability to shed heat years before the same unit would elsewhere — and a system that runs longer, cools less and wears its compressor out early.

The single most useful maintenance task in Baytown is rinsing the outdoor coil, and doing it more often than the rest of the metro — three or four times a year rather than twice. Plain water, garden hose, power off, spraying from the inside out. It rinses the salt off before it can work on the metal.

Coated coils are worth asking about

When replacement time comes, manufacturers offer coastal or corrosion-resistant coil coatings, sometimes as an option and sometimes on a specific model line. In most of Houston that is an upsell. In Baytown it is a reasonable thing to pay for, and the case for it strengthens the closer you are to the water.

Ask each contractor quoting a replacement whether the equipment they propose is available with a coastal coil, and what it adds. Then decide with the number in front of you.

Signs corrosion is already costing you

  • Fins that look chalky or powdery rather than bright metal.
  • White or green deposits where copper meets aluminium.
  • Fins crumbling when brushed gently — at that point the coil is near the end.
  • A system that has slowly got worse over two or three summers with no single failure. That gradual decline is often a coil quietly losing capacity.

Hurricane season, practically

Baytown takes weather. Two things worth knowing about equipment: after a storm surge, an outdoor unit that stood in salt water needs inspection before it is switched on, not after. And after any extended power outage, bring the system back on at the thermostat rather than letting it restart under load when power returns — give it a few minutes.

Before you call

  • Look at the condition of the outdoor coil fins and say what you see.
  • Note whether the decline was sudden or gradual over seasons.
  • Say if the unit has been in storm surge or standing water.
  • Check the filter and both breakers.
