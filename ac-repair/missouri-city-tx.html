Two towns in one, as far as air conditioning goes

Missouri City covers a wide span of housing, and which half you live in changes the conversation entirely.

Quail Valley and the older established neighborhoods date largely from the 1970s. Those houses are on their second or third system, frequently still running original ductwork, and the common problems are duct losses, undersized returns and equipment that was replaced without anyone revisiting what it connects to.

Sienna and the newer master-planned sections are a different machine: tight construction, zoned systems, and the humidity complaint that comes with an oversized unit in a well-sealed house.

When you call anyone, say which part of Missouri City you are in and roughly how old the house is. It genuinely changes what a technician should be looking at first, and it saves diagnostic time you are paying for.

The older-house problem: it is usually the ducts

In Quail Valley and the older streets, new equipment connected to original ductwork is the recurring disappointment — the house cools no better after real money is spent. The mechanics of why, and what to ask a contractor about it, are set out on the Tomball page, and they apply here unchanged.

The newer-house problem: cold and clammy

In Sienna and similar newer sections, the complaint is different. The house reaches temperature fast and still feels damp. That is an oversized system satisfying the thermostat before it has run long enough to pull moisture out of the air.

The fix is longer, lower-output running — a two-stage or variable-speed system, or corrected airflow — rather than more capacity.

Clay soil, common to both

Fort Bend County clay swells and shrinks with the seasons, and over years it tilts condenser pads. A compressor running out of level has poorer oil return and a shorter life, and the movement stresses the refrigerant line set where leaks begin. Check yours by eye against a fence line — resetting a pad is inexpensive compared to what it prevents.

Before you call

  • Say which neighborhood and roughly what year the house was built.
  • Note whether it is one room or the whole house.
  • Say whether it is not cold, or cold but humid.
  • Check the filter, both breakers, and whether the outdoor unit sits level.
