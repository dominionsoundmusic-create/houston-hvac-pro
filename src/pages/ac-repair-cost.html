How much does an air conditioner repair cost in Houston? It depends almost entirely on which part failed. A small electrical part that starts the motors is usually a modest bill. A compressor or an evaporator coil can cost more than many people pay for a used car. This guide lays out published ranges for the common repairs, explains what pushes a Houston bill up or down, and shows you what a fair quote looks like.

First, the honest part. Houston Air & Heating does not fix air conditioners and does not set prices. It is a no-cost phone service that passes your call to an independent local HVAC company, which inspects your system and names its own price. Every figure on this page comes from a named, published source. None of them is the line's price, and real Houston quotes vary with the house, the system and the day.

Published repair cost ranges

Cost guides build their numbers from contractor surveys and project data, so two guides rarely agree to the dollar. That is useful, not a problem: when two sources land in roughly the same place, you have a fair sense of the range. The table puts Angi's Houston estimates next to This Old House's national figures. Both include parts and labor.

For the whole job rather than one part, Angi's 2026 Houston page gives an average HVAC repair of about $345, with a range of $128 to $1,972. Angi's furnace page for Houston shows a similar picture for heating: an average of $323, with most calls between $117 and $572.

These are not this line's prices. The line never quotes or bills anything. The ranges above are published estimates from the sources named, listed at the bottom of this page. The company you are put through to sets its own price after it sees your system, and your quote may be higher or lower.

Why the low end and the high end are so far apart

Two homes can have the same symptom and very different bills. Warm air from the vents might come from a failed capacitor, which is a small part and a short visit. It might also come from a refrigerant leak in the indoor coil, which means finding the leak, replacing the coil, pulling a vacuum and recharging. The symptom does not tell you the price. The diagnosis does.

That is why a careful technician tests before quoting, and why a price given over the phone, before anyone has looked, deserves caution in both directions.

What pushes a Houston repair bill up or down

The part is the biggest factor, but it is not the only one. These are the line items worth asking about before the work starts.

The diagnostic or trip fee

Most companies charge to send someone out and run tests. Ask the amount before the visit, and ask if it comes off the repair bill when you hire them for the fix. Angi notes that many companies apply it that way.

Nights, weekends and holidays

After-hours work usually costs more. Angi's Houston guide says emergency calls can add 50 to 100 percent to hourly rates. If the house is safe and the problem can wait for morning, ask what the difference would be.

Where the equipment sits

Many Houston homes have the indoor unit in the attic. A cramped attic with no walkway, or an air handler over a finished ceiling, takes longer to work in safely, and in summer the heat up there slows everything down. More time means more labor.

Refrigerant type and amount

Refrigerant is priced by the pound. Angi lists R-410A at about $40 to $75 a pound and the older R-22 at $100 to $350, because new R-22 production and imports ended in 2020. A bigger system holds more pounds.

Warranty status

If the part is still under the manufacturer's warranty, you may pay only for labor. Find your installation paperwork or the model and serial numbers before the visit, and ask the company to check coverage.

Size and age of the system

Larger systems use larger, pricier parts. Older units can need parts that are harder to find, and an aging system may need more than one fix in the same visit.

How to read an AC repair quote

A good quote lets you see exactly what you are paying for. Texas rules for air conditioning contractors also require the company's license number on its proposals and invoices, which gives you a quick first check. Before you sign, look for each of these:

  • The failed part, named. "Repair outdoor unit" is not enough. "Replace 45/5 microfarad dual run capacitor" is.
  • How they confirmed it. A reading, a test result or a photo. Ask to see the old part afterward.
  • Labor, separately or as a flat job. Either is normal. You just want to know which.
  • Refrigerant type and pounds. If refrigerant is on the bill, the quote should say which kind, how many pounds, and whether a leak search is included.
  • The diagnostic fee and any credit. Shown on paper, not just mentioned.
  • Warranty on the part and on the labor. How long each lasts, in writing.
  • The license number. Then check it yourself on the state license search.

A note on refrigerant leaks

An air conditioner does not use up refrigerant the way a car burns gas. If the charge is low, it went somewhere, and the Department of Energy is clear that adding more without fixing the leak is not a real repair. A recharge with no leak search looks cheaper on the day and costs more over a summer, because you pay for the same pounds again when they escape.

Ask where the leak is, how it was found, and what fixing it costs compared with replacing the leaking part. If the system still runs on R-22, ask about replacement too. That refrigerant now comes only from recovered and stockpiled supply, which is why recharging an R-22 system has become so costly.

What HVAC technicians earn, and why the bill is higher

People often ask how much HVAC guys charge per hour. Two different numbers get mixed up here. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that heating, air conditioning and refrigeration mechanics and installers earned a median of $29.33 an hour, or $61,010 a year, in May 2025. That is what the worker is paid, not what you are charged.

A company's labor rate has to cover the technician's pay plus the vehicle, fuel, tools, insurance, training, licensing, office staff and the hours spent driving between jobs. Angi's Houston guide puts billed repair labor at roughly $75 to $150 an hour. Many companies skip the hourly rate and quote each repair as a flat price.

Furnace and heating repair costs

Heating repairs follow the same pattern as cooling. Across Houston you will find gas furnaces tucked into attics and closets, plus plenty of heat pumps and electric heat strips inside air handlers. Angi's Houston furnace estimate averages $323 for a typical call. Common heating fixes include a dirty flame sensor, a failed igniter, a bad blower motor or a control board. Gas furnaces tend to cost a little more to repair than electric heat.

If a technician mentions a cracked heat exchanger, that is a safety issue, not just a cost line, because it can let combustion gases into the air you breathe. Ask to see the evidence and read the repair or replace guide before deciding. Should the CO alarm sound at any point, nobody stays inside: get out first, then dial 911. For heating trouble, the furnace repair and heating repair pages explain what the visit involves.

Ways to keep AC repair affordable in Houston

Affordable AC repair in Houston is less about finding a low number and more about not paying for the same problem twice. A few habits help:

  1. Do the safe checks first. A thermostat set to the wrong mode, dead thermostat batteries, a clogged filter or a tripped breaker can each look like a broken system. The AC not cooling guide walks through them.
  2. Change the filter on schedule. According to the Department of Energy, replacing a clogged filter lowers the energy an AC uses by 5 to 15 percent, and steady airflow keeps the coil from icing.
  3. Call during business hours when you safely can. If no one in the house is at risk from the heat, a morning visit can avoid an after-hours premium.
  4. Ask for the repair price before agreeing to anything bigger. If replacement is suggested, ask for both prices in writing.
  5. Keep up with seasonal checkups. ENERGY STAR recommends having the cooling side looked at each spring and the heating side each fall. A tune-up catches a weak capacitor or a slow drain before it fails in August.

When a repair stops making sense

A $300 repair on a seven-year-old system is an easy yes. A $2,500 compressor on a system that is 16 years old and still on R-22 is a different decision. If a quote lands near the top of the ranges above, or if this is the second big repair in two summers, read whether to repair or replace and ask the company to price both options. If you want a sense of what a new system involves, the AC installation page covers that.
