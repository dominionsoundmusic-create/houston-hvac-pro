When you need AC repair in Houston, the first job is figuring out what kind of failure you have. A system that will not start, one that runs but cannot keep up, and one that is leaking water are three very different problems. This page helps you narrow it down, do the checks that are safe for a homeowner, and know what a proper diagnostic visit should include before you pay for one.

Houston Air & Heating is not a contractor. It is a free phone line answered at any hour by an automated assistant. That business sends the technician, prices the repair and bills you directly.

Why Houston air conditioners break when they do

The cooling season here is long and it does not let up. Under the 1991 to 2020 climate normals, Houston's average daily high reaches 90 degrees around May 29 and stays at or above it until about September 18. Actual summers often run hotter than the averages: the National Weather Service Houston/Galveston office counted 145 days at or above 90 degrees in 2025, beating the old record of 142 set in 2011.

That means months of near continuous run time, and Gulf Coast humidity adds a second load: the system has to pull water out of the air, not just heat. Parts that switch on and off thousands of times, like the controls for the compressor and fans, wear out with heavy cycling, as the U.S. Department of Energy notes. Drain lines carry water every day for months. When something gives out, it is usually one worn part that finally failed under load.

One thing that is not a breakdown: on the hottest late afternoons a healthy system may fall a degree or two behind the thermostat, then catch up after sunset. If the air from the vents feels clearly cold and the house recovers overnight, that is usually normal for a Houston August.

Safe checks a homeowner can do

Some no-cooling calls end with something simple. These steps never involve removing a panel, touching wires or handling refrigerant.

  1. Read the thermostat. Mode on Cool, set a few degrees below the room, fan on Auto. A blank screen often just needs new batteries.
  2. Pull the filter. If you cannot see light through it, put in a new one. A clogged filter cuts airflow, and the Department of Energy lists dirty filters among the most common causes of poor cooling.
  3. Check the breakers. The indoor and outdoor units usually have separate breakers. If one has tripped, you may flip it back on a single time. If it flips off again, stop there: repeat trips mean an electrical fault.
  4. Look at the outdoor unit. Clear leaves, grass clippings and overgrown plants from around it without reaching inside. Note whether the top fan spins when the system is calling for cooling.
  5. Look at the indoor unit. Standing water in the pan or a float switch that has tripped can shut the whole system down on purpose. Note it for the technician.

What a good diagnostic visit looks like

A diagnostic visit is the technician's time spent finding the cause. You are paying for measurements, not a guess. A careful technician usually works in this order and tells you what the readings were.

  1. Ask you what happened. When it started, what you noticed, what you already checked. Your notes save time.
  2. Confirm airflow. Filter, blower, indoor coil and return air. Refrigerant readings mean little if the system is starved for air.
  3. Test the electrical side. Components measured against their rated values, motors and compressor checked for proper current draw.
  4. Measure the refrigerant side. Gauge pressures and pipe temperatures checked against the maker's charging data. A low charge always means refrigerant escaped somewhere, so the leak has to be located. Only a technician certified under EPA Section 608 may connect gauges or add refrigerant.
  5. Check the drain. The condensate line flushed and flowing and any float switch tested, which matters a lot in Houston homes with the air handler in the attic.
  6. Explain and price. What failed, why, what fixing it costs in writing, and anything else worth watching.

How long the repair itself takes

Many common fixes happen during the first visit if the part is on hand: a worn electrical component, a cleared drain, a replaced thermostat. A leak search can take longer because the technician has to trace the circuit. Big components, such as an indoor coil or a compressor, may need to be ordered and often take a separate trip. Attic work in summer is slow, hot work and can stretch the clock.

If you are told the part is days away, ask whether anything can get partial cooling going in the meantime, and whether the price changes if it arrives sooner.

Before you approve the repair, ask

Fewer repair calls next summer

Change the filter on schedule through the cooling season. Keep shrubs, mulch and mower clippings away from the outdoor unit. Pick a steady setting: ENERGY STAR's thermostat guidance uses 78 degrees for the hours you are home in summer, and setting the thermostat far lower does not cool the house faster, it only keeps the system running longer. A spring HVAC tune-up, before the first heat wave, gives a technician time to catch a worn part while it is still a small job.
