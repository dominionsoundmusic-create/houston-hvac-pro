AC maintenance in Houston is mostly about timing. A cooling system here may run from spring well into fall in heavy, humid air, then sit while a gas furnace or heat pump takes over for a handful of cold weeks. The best maintenance plan follows that calendar: the right check before each season, a few habits in between, and some preparation for the storms that arrive in the middle of the cooling season.

The Houston maintenance calendar

ENERGY STAR advises a professional check-up ahead of each season, spring for cooling and fall for heating. On the Gulf Coast, that general advice maps onto the year like this.

  1. Late winter into early spring: the cooling check. Book the air conditioning visit before the first stretch of warm, sticky days, while companies still have open calendars. A system checked now has its coil, drain and refrigerant charge looked at before months of heavy running, and a weak part has a chance to show up on a mild day instead of a scorching one.
  2. Late spring: storm season planning. Atlantic hurricane season starts June 1. Take photos of the indoor and outdoor units and their labels, note where the outdoor disconnect and the breaker are, and clear the area around the condenser. The section below covers this in more detail.
  3. Summer: habits, not appointments. This is when filters load up fastest and condensate drains carry the most water. Check the filter monthly, watch the drain line and keep the outdoor unit clear. If something seems wrong, call for a repair rather than waiting for a tune-up date.
  4. Early fall: the heating check. Book the furnace or heat pump visit before the first real cold front, while the air conditioner may still be running in the afternoons. A gas furnace needs its burners, venting and heat exchanger looked at; a heat pump needs its heating mode and backup heat tested.
  5. Winter: be ready for a hard freeze. Houston freezes are rare but serious, as February 2021 showed. Make sure carbon monoxide alarms work if you have gas heat, and know where your system's shutoffs are before the forecast turns.

Why AC maintenance in Houston matters more than the national average suggests

Maintenance advice written for the whole country assumes a few months of cooling. In the Houston area the cooling season is long and the air is humid, so the same machine logs far more running hours and pulls far more water out of the air. Every one of those hours moves air through the filter and across the indoor coil, and every gallon of condensate has to drain away through a small pipe.

The U.S. Department of Energy puts the savings from swapping a clogged filter for a clean one at 5 to 15 percent of the air conditioner's energy use, and it says to check the evaporator coil every year and clean it as needed. ENERGY STAR adds that dirty coils make a system run longer, which raises energy costs and shortens the life of the equipment. Those effects stack up faster when the system rarely gets a day off.

Humidity adds a second job. An air conditioner that is short on airflow or refrigerant may still blow cool air, but it can leave the house clammy. Research from the National Institute of Standards and Technology found that when installation faults leave a house too humid, people tend to turn the thermostat down to feel comfortable, which drives energy use up further. A careful technician looking at airflow and charge is checking comfort as well as efficiency.

HVAC service in Houston also means heating

Plenty of Houston houses have a gas furnace up in the attic or tucked in a hall closet; others use a heat pump or electric heat strips in the air handler. The heating side only runs for part of the year, which is exactly why it deserves a look in fall. Parts that sat idle through summer, like an igniter or a flame sensor, show their problems on the first cold night, and that is when every heating company's phone rings at once.

A heat pump is the exception to the one-visit-per-season idea. It does the heating and the cooling, so it runs nearly all year and benefits from attention in both spring and fall.

Habits that matter most in humid heat

A yearly visit does not replace what you can safely do in between. These are the homeowner tasks that make the biggest difference in a Gulf Coast house. None of them involves refrigerant, wiring, gas or opening an electrical panel.

Check the filter every month

ENERGY STAR says to check the air filter in a central air conditioner, furnace or heat pump once a month and clean or change it as needed. Write the date on the frame when you put a new one in. A filter that looks gray and matted after a few weeks of summer running is normal here; a filter you cannot see light through is overdue.

Watch the condensate drain

ENERGY STAR warns that a plugged condensate drain can cause water damage, affect indoor humidity and breed mold. Know where the drain line ends outside and glance at it on hot afternoons: it should drip. Water in the pan under the attic unit, or a system that has shut itself off, is a sign to call. The AC leaking water guide explains what to look for.

Keep the outdoor unit clear

The condenser has to push heat out into already hot air. Keep shrubs, vines, grass clippings, leaves and stored items away from its sides and top, and do not let a fence or a pile of patio furniture box it in. Turn the system off at the thermostat before working near it.

Notice changes early

A new noise, a room that never cools, a jump in the electric bill or ice on the copper lines all point to a problem a technician should find. The AC not cooling guide lists safe checks to try first: thermostat mode and batteries, the filter, and one breaker reset.

Leave these to a technician: refrigerant, capacitors and other electrical parts, anything inside a gas furnace, and the burners. If you smell gas, leave the house first, then call 911 or your gas utility's emergency number from outside. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone out and call 911.

Hurricane season and power outages: prepare the outdoor unit

Hurricane season runs from June 1 through November 30, right through the hardest months of Houston cooling. Hurricane Beryl in July 2024 and other storms have left large parts of the region without power for days in summer heat. Maintenance cannot prevent an outage, but it can make sure the system comes back cleanly when the power does.

  • Before a storm: clear loose items around the condenser that wind could throw into the fins, and photograph the unit, its label and the indoor equipment for your records and any insurance claim.
  • During an outage: switch the thermostat Off so the system does not try to start the moment power flickers back. If you run a portable generator, keep it outdoors and well away from windows, doors and vents because of carbon monoxide.
  • After power returns: wait a few minutes before turning cooling back on, and listen as it starts. A hum without the fan turning, a burning smell or a breaker that trips again means stop and call.
  • If floodwater reached the unit: do not turn it on. Have a technician inspect any outdoor unit, air handler or furnace that sat in water before it runs again.

Maintenance agreements: what to ask before you sign

Many HVAC companies sell a yearly maintenance agreement, sometimes called a service plan or club membership. It usually bundles a spring and a fall visit with other perks. Some are good value and some are mostly a sales tool, and the paperwork is how you tell the difference.

Questions to ask about an HVAC maintenance agreement and why each one matters
Ask thisWhy it matters
What tasks are done at each visit, in writing?"Tune-up" has no fixed meaning. A written task list lets you compare plans and check the visit against the list.
Does it cover both systems and both seasons?A plan with one visit a year may skip the furnace or the heat pump's heating side entirely.
Is coil cleaning included, or extra?Coil cleaning is often the costliest part of a visit and a common add-on charge.
Will I get the readings from each visit?Measurements written down this year are what make next year's visit meaningful.
How does renewal and cancellation work?Know whether it renews automatically, at what price, and how to cancel.
What happens in a summer breakdown?Ask how the plan treats members when demand is high, and get the answer written into the agreement rather than taken on trust.

The industry has a published minimum for what a maintenance inspection should cover: ANSI/ACCA 4 QM, the Air Conditioning Contractors of America standard for residential HVAC maintenance. Asking whether a plan's visits follow it is a fair question. The tune-up checklist breaks those tasks down so you can compare.
