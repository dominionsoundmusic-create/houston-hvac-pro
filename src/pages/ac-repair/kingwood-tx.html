Kingwood: a planned forest community on Lake Houston

Kingwood grew out of a late 1960s land deal between King Ranch and Friendswood Development Company, then an Exxon subsidiary, and the name combines the two. Development began around 1970 and 1971 on former timberland along the East Fork of the San Jacinto River and the shores of Lake Houston. The developers kept much of the tree canopy and laced the neighborhoods with greenbelts, which gave the community its forest identity. During the 1980s it spread east of US 59.

Houston annexed a commercial strip at the entrance in 1995 and the residential areas, along with Forest Cove, in 1996. The city counts the Kingwood Area super neighborhood at about 30 square miles, with 52,899 residents in 2000 and an estimated 65,084 in 2024. Many homes date from the 1970s through the 1990s, and a few older subdivisions predate the master plan altogether.

What the forest does to a Kingwood condenser

The outdoor unit works by pushing heat out through thin aluminum fins. Anything that blocks those fins makes it work harder. In Kingwood, the trees supply a steady stream of things that do exactly that.

Pine needles and straw

Needles land on the top grille, slip inside and mat against the coil. A unit can look fine from the yard while its coil is half covered. Ask that the top be checked and the coil cleaned at each tune-up.

Pollen and catkins

Spring pollen and oak tassels coat the fins in a fine layer. The system still runs, but it sheds heat poorly just as the first hot weeks arrive. A spring cleaning before summer pays off here.

Limbs and storms

A branch over the unit drops debris all year and can come down on it in a storm. Keep limbs trimmed back and leave about two feet clear on every side so air can move.

City of Houston permits apply in Kingwood

Because Kingwood is inside Houston's city limits, a system replacement there is handled through the Houston Permitting Center. The city requires a permit for the installation, repair or replacement of a condensing unit, the outdoor half of a central AC. The permit calls for an inspection, and a licensed contractor applying for it must first register its state license with the City of Houston. Questions about which permit fits a particular job go to the city's mechanical section at 832-394-8850.

For you, that means two simple questions before you sign a replacement quote: does the price include the city permit and inspection, and what is the company's Texas ACR license number? A company that works inside Houston regularly should be able to answer both without hesitation. You can confirm the license yourself on TDLR's site.

Older Kingwood systems and the repair or replace question

A house built in the late 1970s or 1980s is likely on its second or third air conditioner, and the ducts in the attic may be original. If yours is older and still running on R-22, the refrigerant that stopped being produced or imported in the United States at the start of 2020, any leak becomes expensive because the remaining supply is limited. That is usually when replacement makes more sense than another repair. Ask the technician what refrigerant your system uses and whether the ducts were checked before anyone sizes a new unit. The repair or replace guide walks through the decision.

Harvey, the river and where you put the next unit

Hurricane Harvey hit Kingwood harder than almost anywhere in the Houston area. In the weeks after the August 2017 storm, estimates reported by ABC13 put the damage at more than 300 businesses and as many as 5,000 homes, more than one in ten homes in Kingwood. Many residents blamed the release of water from the Lake Conroe dam upstream; the San Jacinto River Authority has said the release reduced, rather than worsened, the flooding. Either way, the river and the lake sit right at the edge of the community.

If your street took water, two habits help. First, never run an outdoor unit or an indoor unit that has been under water until a technician has inspected it. Second, when the system is due for replacement, ask about a raised stand or platform for the new condenser. It costs much less to do during a replacement than as a separate job.

Safe checks for a Kingwood home

  • Thermostat set to Cool, below the room temperature, with working batteries.
  • Air filter. Replace it if it is gray and dusty; spring pollen loads filters quickly here.
  • Breaker. Reset a tripped AC breaker one time. If it trips again, call.
  • Outdoor unit. Rake away pine straw and leaves and pull back any shrubs crowding it. Do not take the unit apart.
  • Drain. Look for water at the indoor unit or a tripped float switch, which shuts the system down when the drain backs up.

Refrigerant, electrical parts inside the unit and anything involving gas are technician work. A gas smell means leaving the house first and calling the gas utility's emergency line or 911 from outside. A carbon monoxide alarm means getting everyone out and calling 911.

Power in Kingwood

Kingwood is in CenterPoint Energy's delivery area, and like the rest of Houston it is in the competitive electric market. CenterPoint owns the poles, wires and meters and restores outages, while your chosen retail provider handles billing. Neither touches air conditioners, so if outlets work and the AC does not, it is an HVAC call.
