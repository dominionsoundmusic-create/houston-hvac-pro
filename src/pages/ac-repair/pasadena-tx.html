Pasadena: from strawberry fields to the Ship Channel

Pasadena was laid out in 1893 by John H. Burnett, a Galveston businessman, who borrowed the name of the California city for the green, lush look of the land. After the 1900 Galveston hurricane, the American Red Cross under Clara Barton bought 1.5 million strawberry plants for Gulf Coast farmers, and Pasadena became the strawberry capital of the region.

The Houston Ship Channel changed everything. By 1920 the Sinclair, Texaco and Crown oil companies had built refineries in the area, and after World War II the channel became a center of the petrochemical industry. Pasadena went from 3,436 residents in 1940 to 22,483 in 1950 and 58,737 in 1960, according to the Handbook of Texas. Those plant workers needed houses fast, and a large share of the city's streets were built in those two decades.

A Pasadena house by era: what to ask the technician about

A rough guide only. Remodels and past replacements change the picture, so let the company confirm what is in your attic, closet and side yard.

Built in the 1940s to 1960s boom

Many of these homes were not designed around central air, so the air handler and ducts were fitted in later wherever they would go. Ask whether the return air opening is big enough for the system and whether long duct runs to the back bedrooms leak.

Built in the 1970s and 1980s

Central air from the start, but the original equipment is long gone and the ducts may be the same age as the roof. Ask whether a replacement is being sized to the house, with a load calculation, rather than copied from the old unit's label.

East of 146, built or changed after March 1, 1997

For homes in Pasadena's windstorm area, TDI ties its inspection rules to construction after that date. Ask whether the outdoor unit will be anchored to its pad and whether the job will be inspected for a windstorm certificate.

The windstorm rule that applies to only part of Pasadena

Pasadena is unusual among Harris County suburbs. The Texas Department of Insurance treats the part of the city that lies east of State Highway 146, inside the city limits, as a designated catastrophe area, the same status as the first tier counties on the coast. TDI first added that part of Pasadena in 1997, along with nearby Shoreacres.

Why does that matter for air conditioning? To qualify for wind and hail coverage through the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association, new work and repairs in a catastrophe area must meet TDI building specifications and pass inspection, and TDI's own program materials list air conditioner units and mechanical equipment among the items that need certification. In practice the focus is on the outdoor condenser: TDI's product rules call for a pad big enough that the unit rests fully on it and can be fastened down. A loose condenser can shift in a hurricane, kink its refrigerant lines or turn into flying debris.

If your house is on the bay side of 146, raise this before you sign for a new system. Ask the company whether the unit will be anchored to the windstorm standard, who arranges the TDI or engineer inspection, and whether you will get the certificate for your records. If you live west of 146, which covers most of Pasadena, the windstorm program does not apply to you.

City permits for AC work in Pasadena

The City of Pasadena Permit Department, on the first floor of City Hall at 1149 Ellsworth Drive, handles mechanical permits. Its published fee schedule lists separate entries for a complete heating or air conditioning installation or replacement, priced by tonnage, and for replacing only a condenser, an air handler or a heater. Building and remodel contractors register with the department before they can pull permits.

The city has also tightened its energy paperwork. It adopted the 2024 Uniform Mechanical Code and the 2024 International Energy Conservation Code with local amendments, and it says that starting February 2, 2026 it requires energy code compliance documentation, including heat load calculations for new construction and complete remodels. For a homeowner the takeaway is short: a full system change is permitted, inspected work, so the quote you sign should name the permit and the final inspection.

Quick checks before you call

  • Thermostat. Set to Cool, a few degrees below room temperature, with fresh batteries if it uses them.
  • Air filter. Lint and dust can smother a filter until the indoor coil ices over. Put in a clean one if yours looks matted.
  • Breaker. Flip the AC breaker back on once if it has tripped. Should it pop again, something is wrong, so stop there and phone for help.
  • Outdoor unit. Pull back grass clippings, leaves and anything stacked against the condenser so it can move air.

Anything past that, including refrigerant, wiring and the parts inside the panels, is a job for a technician. Our guide to an AC that is not cooling walks through the common causes.
