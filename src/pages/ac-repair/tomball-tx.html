Tomball: a small city with a big rural edge

Tomball is an incorporated city in northwest Harris County with its own city government, set along State Highway 249, which locals call Tomball Parkway. The city itself is compact, but the Tomball name on mailing addresses reaches well beyond its limits into wooded subdivisions and acreage in unincorporated parts of the county.

That spread is the most practical thing to know about cooling and heating here. A home in town and a home on acreage a few miles out may share a ZIP code and still differ on who issues permits, whether natural gas reaches the house, and how far a technician has to drive.

The town began as a rail stop called Peck and took the name Tomball in 1907, honoring Thomas Ball, the Houston lawyer and congressman credited with bringing the railroad through. Oil was struck nearby in 1933, the same year the city incorporated. The city notes that Tomball sits at the highest point in Harris County, which is why the railroad liked it: loaded trains could roll downhill toward the coast.

A growing town with its own gas system

The 2020 Census counted 12,341 people in the City of Tomball, up from 10,753 in 2010. The Census Bureau's estimate for July 2025 is 15,768, so the city is adding residents faster than it did in the last decade, and the unincorporated land around it with Tomball addresses is growing too. New houses mean new systems still under warranty sitting next to older homes on their second or third air conditioner.

  • Natural gas inside city limitsThe City of Tomball owns and runs the gas system within the city and bills it on the city utility account, so a gas leak or meter question in town goes to the city, not a private gas company.
  • ElectricityThe city does not sell power. It points residents to the state's Power to Choose site to pick a retail electric provider, and CenterPoint Energy lists Tomball in its electric delivery area, so CenterPoint owns the poles and wires and handles outages.
  • CountyTomball is in northwest Harris County. Acreage outside the city falls under Harris County, with no city building department.
  • Permits in townCity of Tomball permit forms say heating, ventilation and air conditioning work needs its own permit, separate from a building permit.

Inside city limits or outside: what changes

Not sure which side you are on? Your property tax statement lists every taxing unit for your home, and the City of Tomball appears there only if you are inside its limits.

Inside the City of Tomball

The city has its own building rules, and its mechanical permit application covers heating and air conditioning work. The form warns that skipping a required permit brings penalty fees of double the permit cost, and that a permit lapses if work has not started within six months. Ask the HVAC company to pull the permit and list it on the quote.

Unincorporated, Tomball address

New equipment, old ducts

A common Tomball story: an older house gets a new outdoor unit and the back bedroom is still warm. Ducts that were run decades ago may leak at the joints, sag in the attic or feed air back through a return that is too small for today's equipment. A correctly sized system cannot fix air that never reaches the room. When you collect replacement quotes, ask each company:

  • Did you measure the house? A load calculation looks at the actual home, not the old unit's size.
  • What about the ducts and the return? Ask whether they were inspected and what the quote does about them.
  • Are the indoor and outdoor parts matched? A new outdoor unit paired with an old indoor coil can cause repeat problems.
  • Is there a trip charge? For a home on acreage outside town, ask before the visit.
  • May I have your ACR license number? Run it through the TDLR search before anyone starts.

Outdoor units on big lots

A condenser on an acre or more lives a rougher life than one tucked between two suburban fences. Riding mowers and string trimmers throw grass and gravel at the coil. Vines and brush creep in from the tree line within a season. Long runs of refrigerant line and wiring from a detached shop or a house set far back can be nicked by equipment or chewed by animals. None of that is a reason to open the unit yourself, but it is worth a look when you mow:

  • Keep a clear ring of a couple of feet around the outdoor unit, and point mower discharge away from it.
  • Watch the insulation on the larger refrigerant line. If it is torn or missing, mention it to the company you reach.
  • Note anything odd such as a burnt smell, a bent fan guard or a pad that has sunk and tilted, and describe it on the call.

Before you call: quick checks

Make sure the thermostat is on the right mode with a sensible setting, swap a filter that is gray with dust, and give a tripped breaker a single reset. On a wooded or rural lot, look for leaves, grass clippings or vines packed against the outdoor unit, and clear them by hand with the system off. If the breaker trips again or the unit hums without starting, stop and call. Long outages after storms such as Hurricane Beryl in July 2024 are also a good reason to learn where your breaker panel is before you need it.
