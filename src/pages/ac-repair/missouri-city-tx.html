Missouri City: a railroad land sale that became a city of 74,000

In 1890 two Houston investors bought land along the rail line and marketed it to buyers in St. Louis as "a land of genial sunshine and eternal summer." The name Missouri City followed in 1893. By the 1950s it was a bedroom community for people working in Houston, and in March 1956 residents incorporated, partly out of fear that Houston would annex them.

The city is mostly in Fort Bend County, with a small part in Harris County. It counted 74,259 people in the 2020 census, up from 67,358 in 2010. Unlike Houston, it has had zoning since 1981. Its housing came in waves: Fondren Park near US 90A in the early 1960s, Quail Valley starting in 1969, and newer planned sections such as Sienna later on.

Two kinds of Missouri City house

A rough guide only. Remodels and earlier replacements change the picture, so a technician needs to see your own equipment before drawing conclusions.

Quail Valley and the older streets

Quail Valley homes went up mostly between 1969 and the end of the 1970s. After roughly 50 Gulf Coast summers, most are well past their first air conditioner, and the ducts in the attic may never have been replaced. Ask whether the ducts were checked for leaks before anyone sizes new equipment, and whether the old system uses R-22.

Sienna and the newer sections

Newer houses are usually tighter and larger, often with a second system upstairs. The common complaint is a house that cools fast but feels damp, which can point to a system too big to run long enough to pull moisture out. Ask about airflow settings and system staging before anyone sells you more tonnage.

What Harvey taught Missouri City homeowners

Hurricane Harvey hit Missouri City twice over in August 2017. A tornado on August 26 tore roofs and knocked down walls and trees, and in Sienna alone one tornado damaged an estimated 75 to 100 homes. Then the rain came. The mayor reported about 100 homes taking water in the Lake Olympia subdivision, with water also backing up in Quail Valley and Sienna, while the Brazos River at Richmond crested above 55 feet.

Both kinds of damage reach an air conditioner. Wind throws debris into condenser fins and can knock a unit off its pad or bend its refrigerant lines. Floodwater gets into wiring, controls and the indoor unit. If your system went through either and has not been looked at since, ask for an inspection on a mild day rather than finding out during a heat wave.

Before you call: safe checks only

  • Make sure the thermostat is on Cool with a setting a few degrees under the current indoor temperature. Change the batteries if the display is faint.
  • Pull out the air filter. If it is gray and matted, replace it.
  • If the AC breaker has tripped, flip it back one time. Should it trip a second time, stop there.
  • Once a storm passes, view the outdoor unit from a safe distance. Tilted, dented or tangled in debris means leave it off and say so on the call.

If you smell gas, get out of the house first, then call 911 and the gas company named on your bill. If the carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone outside and call 911.

Power and permits in Missouri City

Missouri City is in CenterPoint Energy's delivery area with retail electric choice, like the rest of this part of Fort Bend County. CenterPoint handles outages and lines; your retail provider handles the bill. Lights on but no cooling means the fault is in the equipment, which is HVAC company territory.

The city's permits office takes applications through the MCTX Self Service portal, and its mechanical permit form lists a $35 application fee plus duct system fees. Inspections cannot be booked until a permit has been issued. Ask the company whether it will apply for the permit, and get that in writing.
