Sugar Land: from Imperial Sugar's company town to a city of 111,000

The land here was granted to Samuel May Williams by Stephen F. Austin in 1828, and Williams planted sugarcane. In the early 1900s the Imperial Sugar Company ran the place as a company town, providing housing, schools, stores and doctors. Only after World War II did the company start selling homes and land, with employees first in line. Sugar Land incorporated in 1959, and the original refinery closed in 2003.

The 2020 census counted 111,026 residents, up from 78,817 in 2010. The city credits that jump to the completion of Telfair, building in Lake Pointe, and the December 2017 annexation of Greatwood and New Territory. So a Sugar Land system might sit in an older home near the old refinery or in a house from the last decade, and the right questions differ for each.

Levee districts and the Brazos River

Large parts of Sugar Land sit inside levee improvement districts, local government bodies set up to provide flood protection and drainage and to reclaim land from the floodplain.

Harvey, 2017

As the Brazos rose, the city ordered mandatory evacuations for Levee Improvement Districts 1 and 7. About 230 homes took on up to 6 inches of water, mostly around Settlers Park and Chimney Stone.

The levees held

An engineer working with the districts said no Fort Bend County levee breached or overtopped. Officials pointed instead to more than 30 inches of rain overwhelming interior drainage while the river was high.

Since then

Districts have added pumping. In Riverstone, two levee districts built a $9.1 million pump project at Steep Bank Creek, their biggest flood protection project since Harvey.

What the levee setting means for your equipment

Living behind a levee is not the same as living on high ground. When heavy rain falls while the Brazos is up, water inside the levee has to be pumped out, and low spots can pond. An outdoor condenser sitting on a low pad at the back of a lot is the part of your system most exposed to that.

If water ever reached your condenser, air handler or the electrical disconnect beside the unit, do not just flip it back on. Ask a technician to check the wiring, controls and coil first. If you are replacing a system anyway, it is fair to ask whether the new condenser can sit higher on your lot.

Humid air, big homes and two systems

Sugar Land's planned communities are full of two-story houses, and many of them use one system for each floor. The upstairs unit usually works harder, because heat rises and the attic above it bakes all summer. If one floor is fine and the other is warm, tell the company which floor, since it narrows the search right away.

Gulf Coast humidity also changes how comfort feels. An air conditioner pulls moisture out of the air as it runs. A unit that is too large for the house reaches the set temperature in short bursts and shuts off before it dries the air, leaving rooms cool but clammy. If that sounds like your house, mention it before anyone quotes a bigger replacement.

A few safe checks first

  • Confirm each thermostat is on Cool and set below the current room temperature. Replace weak batteries.
  • Look at the filter for each system. Two systems usually means two filters.
  • Reset a tripped breaker one time only. A second trip means stop and call.
  • Clear leaves and mulch away from the outdoor unit.

Gas or carbon monoxide. If you smell gas, leave the house first, then call 911 and the gas company listed on your bill from outside. If a carbon monoxide alarm goes off, get everyone out and call 911.

Power and permits in Sugar Land

Sugar Land is in CenterPoint Energy's delivery area and in the retail choice market. CenterPoint runs the wires and restores outages, while the retail provider you signed up with handles billing. Air conditioners are outside both of their jobs, so if the outlets have power while the AC sits idle, phone an HVAC company.

The city's homeowner permit brochure lists repairing, replacing or adding an HVAC system, and work on duct systems, as jobs that need a permit. Ask the company to include the permit and the inspection in its written quote. That keeps the work on record if you sell the house later.

Questions worth asking

  • What is your Texas ACR license number? Confirm it on TDLR's site before anyone starts.
  • Will you pull the Sugar Land permit? And will you be there for the inspection?
  • Which system is the problem? In a two-system house, get a separate diagnosis for each.
  • Is the new unit sized for this house? Ask for a load calculation, not a copy of the old size.
  • Does the system run on R-22? Production and imports of that refrigerant ended in the U.S. in 2020, which affects repair costs.
