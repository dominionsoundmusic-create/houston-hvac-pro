Heating repair in Houston is mostly an electric story. Some homes here burn gas, but a large share warm up with a heat pump or with electric resistance coils built into the air handler. Those systems fail in different ways from a furnace, and some of their normal habits look like breakdowns. This page explains how they work, what common symptoms mean, how freezes and grid stress affect them, and when it is time to talk about replacement.

How Houston homes heat

Texas heats with electricity far more than the country as a whole. A 2021 EIA analysis of Census figures found electricity was the primary heat source in 61 percent of Texas homes, compared with 39 percent nationally. Three kinds of systems cover nearly every house in greater Houston, and you can usually tell them apart in a minute.

What a heat pump does on a Houston winter day

A heat pump does not create heat by burning anything. It pulls warmth out of the outdoor air, even chilly air, and carries it inside. That is why Houston's mild winters suit it, and why it works harder on the few nights that drop well below freezing. A Department of Energy Building America field study notes that standard air-source heat pumps lose heating capacity faster once it falls below about 30 degrees outside and lean more and more on supplemental electric heat.

On damp, cold mornings, frost can coat the outdoor coil. To clear it, the system runs a defrost cycle: Department of Energy training material describes the heat pump periodically switching into cooling mode so warm refrigerant melts the ice, usually for about 5 to 15 minutes. The outdoor fan may stop, you may see a cloud of steam, and the indoor unit typically turns on its electric strips so the air coming from the vents does not feel cold. A few minutes later, normal heating resumes.

Aux heat and emergency heat, in plain terms

Aux on the thermostat is the system calling for its backup strips by itself, during defrost or when the heat pump alone cannot keep up. Emergency heat is a manual switch that turns the heat pump off and runs only the strips. DOE guidance calls electric resistance heat inefficient and advises keeping its use to a minimum, and it recommends thermostats designed for heat pumps, which raise the temperature gradually so the strips are not called in for every small change. If you have been on emergency heat for days, or Aux shows up on mild afternoons, the heat pump itself probably needs attention.

Electric heat strips draw a lot of current. A breaker that trips repeatedly, a hot plastic smell or scorched wiring at the air handler is a reason to turn the system off at the thermostat and wait for a professional, not to keep resetting it.

Freezes, the Texas grid and electric heat

Because so many homes here run on electric heat, a Houston freeze hits the power grid and the furnace closet at once. EIA notes that the ERCOT grid serving most of Texas has limited connections to the rest of the country, so it relies mainly on power generated inside the state. In February 2021 that grid could not keep up. A University of Houston Hobby School survey afterward found that 69 percent of Texans lost electricity at some point during the storm, for an average of 42 hours, and nearly half lost running water too.

For a heat pump home, an outage means no heat at all, and the hours right after power returns are hard on equipment: thousands of strips and compressors start at once, often in the coldest part of the night. A few habits help:

  • Lower the thermostat a few degrees during a cold snap so the strips cycle less.
  • Skip emergency heat unless the heat pump has actually failed; Aux will step in on its own.
  • Keep leaves, trash cans and drifting debris away from the outdoor unit so it can breathe and drain its defrost water.
  • Each fall, run the heat once on a cool evening and change the filter; ENERGY STAR suggests a fall checkup for heating before contractors get busy.
  • After an outage, give the system a few minutes once power is back. If it runs but blows cool, or a breaker trips, call instead of resetting it again and again.

When replacing the heating side makes sense

In a Houston home the heating and cooling equipment are often the same machine or share one air handler, so a heating breakdown can open a bigger question. Repair usually wins when a single part fails on equipment in decent shape. Replacement deserves a look when:

Repair is usually reasonable

A burned-out heat strip, a failed sequencer, a defrost sensor or a fan motor on a system that otherwise heats and cools well, with no history of refrigerant leaks.

Get a replacement price too

The compressor or coil has failed on an aging heat pump, refrigerant leaks keep returning, the home has never heated evenly, or you have been living on emergency heat because the heat pump no longer works.

Inside City of Houston limits, replacing heating equipment needs a mechanical permit pulled by a contractor licensed by TDLR and registered with the city, followed by an inspection. Ask whether the price includes it. The repair or replace guide walks through the decision, and the AC installation and replacement page covers what a new system involves.

What a careful heating technician checks

  • Runs the system in heating mode and confirms the outdoor unit is really producing heat, not just the strips
  • Forces a defrost cycle to prove the defrost board, sensor and reversing valve work
  • Measures the amp draw of each heat strip so you know how many still work
  • Checks airflow, the filter and the blower, since poor airflow trips heat limits
  • Explains the finding and gives a written price before starting any repair
