Commercial HVAC in Houston, TX lives a harder life than a home system. Rooftop units sit in direct sun over sales floors, kitchens add heat and grease, and buildings full of people and equipment need moisture pulled out of the air for most of the year. When a unit fails, the cost is counted in lost customers, spoiled stock and staff sent home. This page explains the equipment most Houston businesses run on, how commercial service differs from residential, the Texas license classes and City of Houston rules that apply, the federal refrigerant rules owners carry, and the questions to ask before you sign a work order or a maintenance agreement.

How commercial service differs from a house call

Plenty of companies do both kinds of work, but the equipment, the licensing and the paperwork are not the same. These are the differences that change what you should ask.

What sits on most Houston commercial roofs

Strip centers along the freeways, office parks and big-box stores across the Houston area are mostly cooled by packaged rooftop units, usually shortened to RTUs. One steel cabinet holds the compressor, both coils, the blower and often a gas heat section, set on a curb directly over the space it serves. A small retail suite may have one. A school or supermarket may have dozens.

Many RTUs carry an economizer, a set of dampers and controls that brings in outdoor air for free cooling when conditions allow. Pacific Northwest National Laboratory's training material on economizers explains how they reduce or eliminate mechanical cooling in mild weather. On the Gulf Coast the danger is the opposite case: a damper stuck open or a failed sensor can pour hot, wet outdoor air into the building all afternoon, and the complaint sounds exactly like a weak compressor. A careful technician checks the economizer before condemning anything expensive.

Older buildings and small professional suites often run split systems that look like a big residential setup. Server rooms and additions frequently get ductless units of their own. High-rise offices and hospital and campus buildings move up to chilled water plants, which is specialty work. If you know what equipment serves the space, say so on the call so the company can plan.

Common problems by type of business

Restaurants and commercial kitchens

Hoods remove a large volume of air, and that air has to be replaced. When the makeup air unit stops, the building goes into negative pressure: doors pull hard, smoke drifts and the dining room draws in humid outdoor air through every gap faster than the rooftop units can treat it. Grease also coats coils near the kitchen. Ask for the hood, the makeup air and the cooling to be looked at together.

Offices and medical suites

The trouble is usually one area: a corner that faces west, a conference room packed every afternoon, an exam room that never cools. Causes include stuck zone dampers, a thermostat on the wrong wall, or a layout rebuilt for a new tenant without changing the ducts.

Retail and showrooms

Doors open all day and glass storefronts take the full afternoon sun. Every opening brings moisture in, so a store can feel clammy even when the thermostat reads right. Dirty condenser coils and failed fan motors show up quickly in higher bills and warm aisles.

Warehouses and flex space

Often only the office and break room are air conditioned, while the floor is ventilated or heated. Dock doors and forklifts load filters and coils with dust, so service intervals tend to be shorter than a calendar schedule suggests.

Server and network closets need a word of their own. ASHRAE's recommended range for air entering IT equipment runs from 64.4 to 80.6 degrees Fahrenheit, so the room does not have to be cold, but it does need cooling around the clock. A closet that relies on the building system loses cooling every night and weekend the thermostat sets back. A dedicated unit with a high temperature alarm is the usual fix.

Texas license classes for commercial HVAC work

The Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR) licenses air conditioning and refrigeration contractors, and on commercial jobs the class on that license matters. TDLR describes a Class B license as covering cooling systems of 25 tons and under and heating systems of 1.5 million BTUs per hour and under, with no limit for Class A. In a building with several units, a Class B contractor may work on each one as long as that single unit stays within those limits. A typical strip center RTU falls well inside Class B. A big central plant does not.

TDLR also issues endorsements for environmental air conditioning (comfort cooling), commercial refrigeration and process cooling or heating. If the problem is a walk-in cooler, reach-in case or ice machine rather than the comfort system, ask whether the company holds the commercial refrigeration endorsement.

Insurance is a state requirement too. TDLR's certificate of insurance form sets general liability minimums of $100,000 per occurrence and $200,000 aggregate for Class B, and $300,000 per occurrence and $600,000 aggregate for Class A, including coverage for completed work. Ask for a certificate naming your business before anyone climbs the ladder, and confirm the license yourself. The line does not check either one.

City of Houston rules and federal refrigerant records

  • CodeSince January 2024 the City of Houston has enforced the 2021 Uniform Mechanical Code with local amendments. Suburban cities such as Sugar Land, Pasadena and Pearland adopt their own codes and run their own permit offices.
  • Contractor registrationA contractor holding a state air conditioning license must register it with the City of Houston before working inside city limits. The registration is renewed every year and includes a certificate of insurance naming the licensee.
  • PermitsThe Houston Permitting Center requires a permit to install, repair or replace a condensing unit on commercial or residential property. Inspection is required and plan review may be, depending on scope. The City's Mechanical Section answers questions at 832-394-8850.
  • Section 608Federal law bans knowingly venting refrigerant during service, repair or disposal, and anyone who handles it must be EPA certified.
  • Leak repairOwners must repair leaks once a system passes a leak rate threshold: 10 percent a year for comfort cooling and 20 percent for commercial refrigeration. The rule has long covered equipment with 50 pounds or more of older ozone-depleting refrigerant; since January 2026 it also reaches equipment with 15 pounds or more of most HFCs.
  • RecordsEPA's HFC rules ask owners and operators of covered equipment to keep records for three years. Ask your contractor for a copy of every service report that shows refrigerant added.

Preventive maintenance agreements

A written maintenance agreement is how most businesses cut down on emergency calls. ANSI/ASHRAE/ACCA Standard 180 lays out minimum inspection and maintenance tasks for commercial HVAC systems, aimed at keeping comfort, efficiency and indoor air quality where they should be. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory's maintenance guide for rooftop units makes the case for owners in plain terms: maintenance is essential for efficiency, reliability and lower operating cost.

Get these in writing

  • How many visits a year, and in which months
  • The task list for each visit: filters, belts, coils, condensate drains, electrical readings, economizer and controls
  • Whether parts, refrigerant and after-hours calls are included or billed separately
  • A short written report for each unit after every visit
  • The TDLR license number and class, and a current certificate of insurance

Reasons to pause

  • An automatic multi-year renewal with no simple exit
  • "Unlimited service" with no list of exclusions
  • A replacement pushed at the first visit with no repair price beside it
  • No clear answer on who keeps the refrigerant records
  • Reluctance to name the license class

Condensate drains deserve a line of their own in Houston. Rooftop and indoor coils produce a steady stream of water through the humid months, and a clogged drain can mean a wet ceiling tile over a sales floor or a soaked office. Ask how often drains and pans are cleared and whether overflow switches are checked.

Planning for hurricane season and power outages

Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 through November 30, which overlaps the hottest part of the Houston year. Hurricane Beryl in July 2024 showed how long a business can be without power after a storm, and how hard every HVAC company is to reach once power comes back and thousands of systems need attention at once. A little planning before the season helps.

  • Book the spring visit early. Have rooftop units checked before the heat and the storms, while schedules are open.
  • Ask about the roof. Have the technician note loose access panels, damaged curbs or unsecured equipment during maintenance, and report them to whoever owns the roof.
  • Know your restart order. After an outage, bringing every unit back at once can trip breakers. Ask your contractor what order makes sense for your building.
  • Plan for the server room. If you run a generator, ask whether it is sized to carry the closet's cooling, not only the computers.
  • Keep the list handy. Unit locations, model numbers, the last service report and the lease clause on HVAC, all in one place.

What to ask a commercial HVAC company in Houston

Whether you reach a company through the line or find one yourself, these questions help separate a contractor set up for commercial work from one that mostly works on houses.

  1. What is your TDLR license number and class? Make sure the class covers your largest unit, then look it up.
  2. Is your license registered with the City of Houston? It must be for work inside city limits, and the company pulls the permit.
  3. Who on the crew holds EPA certification? Anyone handling refrigerant must.
  4. May we have a certificate of insurance? Before anyone goes on the roof.
  5. Which of our units fall under EPA leak repair rules? And will you send copies of each service record?
  6. Can the work be done around our hours? Ask how after-hours labor is priced.
  7. If replacement comes up: Was the new unit sized from a load calculation, does it fit the existing curb, will it need a crane and a permit, and how long is the labor warranty apart from the equipment warranty? New light commercial air conditioners made since January 1, 2025 must use a refrigerant with a global warming potential under 700 under EPA rules, so a new unit may differ from the old one in more than size.

Before you call: what to have ready

  • Which unit serves the problem area. If units are not labeled, note which thermostat controls that space.
  • What the thermostat or controls show. Mode, setpoint, schedule and whether it thinks the building is occupied. A holiday schedule that never ended is a common cause of a "broken" system.
  • The breaker. A tripped breaker can be reset once. If it trips again, leave it off for the technician.
  • Service history. Who serviced the units last and when filters were changed.
  • Roof access. Where the hatch or ladder is, and whether a key, escort or landlord approval is needed.
  • The lease. Many commercial leases put rooftop repairs on the tenant. Check before approving a large job.

Utility incentives for business equipment

CenterPoint Energy, the electric delivery company across much of the Houston area, runs commercial and industrial efficiency programs, and has offered incentives for qualifying higher efficiency equipment through participating contractors and distributors. Rules, eligible equipment and funding change from year to year. Ask the company quoting your replacement whether a CenterPoint program is open and whether your equipment would qualify before it is ordered.
