Air conditioning repair in Houston goes better when you know what the parts of your system do. Central air here has two jobs, cooling the house and drying it, and the parts that do those jobs wear out in fairly predictable ways. This guide walks through each component, what failure looks like, and what the repair actually involves, so the technician's explanation makes sense and the price can be weighed.

How central air works in Gulf Coast humidity

A split system has an outdoor unit (the condenser, with the compressor and a large fan) and an indoor unit (the air handler or furnace, with a blower and the evaporator coil). Refrigerant flowing between them picks up heat from your indoor air at the cold coil and releases it outside.

In Houston, the cold coil does something just as important: it wrings water out of the air. Moisture condenses on the coil, drips into a pan and leaves through the condensate drain. That only works when the system runs long enough. ENERGY STAR warns that an oversized unit short cycles, so less moisture drains off the coil and the air is left damp, and Department of Energy research notes that correct sizing is especially important in humid climates.

So a Houston system can fail in two ways: it can stop cooling, or it can keep cooling while losing its grip on humidity. Sticky air at a normal temperature, musty smells and sweating supply vents are worth mentioning on the call, because they point the technician toward airflow, coil and sizing questions rather than a broken part.

The parts Houston heat is hardest on

The Department of Energy lists frequent on and off cycling as a cause of electrical control failure, and names refrigerant leaks, skipped maintenance such as dirty filters, and drainage problems among the most common air conditioner troubles. Here those stresses stack up. A cooling season that runs from spring into fall means heavy cycling for months, and a coil that sheds water every day keeps the drain line and pan busy.

Electrical starting and switching parts

Capacitors and contactors sit inside a metal cabinet in direct sun. They are small, inexpensive parts relative to the system, which is why a correct diagnosis matters: replacing one is a modest job, but only if it is truly the part that failed. These are not homeowner repairs. A capacitor can hold a dangerous charge even with the power off.

Water handling

Algae and sludge build up in condensate lines over a long, wet season. When the main line clogs, a float switch may shut the system down to protect the ceiling, which can look like a total breakdown. Clearing the line and testing the switch is usually a short visit.

Attic equipment and ducts

Many Houston homes have the air handler and most of the ductwork in the attic. Leaky ducts, and return air pulled in from a hot attic, add heat and humidity the system then has to remove. A disconnected or crushed flex duct shows up as one room that never cools, and a technician can usually find it with a flashlight and a temperature reading at each vent.

Ask whether the duct repair includes sealing the joints with mastic or tape rated for the job, not just reattaching them.

Refrigerant rules that shape your repair options

Refrigerant work is the part of air conditioning repair where federal rules matter most, and they affect what a fair quote looks like.

  • Only certified technicians open the system.Federal Clean Air Act rules (Section 608) say anyone who attaches gauges, adds or removes refrigerant, or replaces a sealed system part must hold EPA Section 608 certification, and only certified technicians may buy refrigerant for home systems. Knowingly venting refrigerant is illegal.
  • Low refrigerant means a leak.Refrigerant is not used up like fuel. The EPA advises homeowners to have leaks found and repaired rather than paying to top off a leaking system again and again.
  • R-22 is in its final chapter.U.S. production and import of new R-22 ended on January 1, 2020. Existing R-22 systems can still be serviced with recovered and reclaimed refrigerant, but the EPA expects that supply to keep getting more expensive.
  • R-410A systems can still be repaired.EPA rules under the AIM Act cap new residential systems made or imported from January 1, 2025 at refrigerants with a global warming potential below 700, and R-410A equipment already in homes remains legal to repair and recharge. A final EPA rule from May 2026 removed the January 1, 2026 deadline for installing R-410A equipment made before 2025, so that older stock can be installed until it runs out.
  • New systems use A2L refrigerants.The EPA noted in 2025 that most major manufacturers had already moved new residential equipment to R-454B, a mildly flammable (A2L) refrigerant. If you are quoted a new system, ask which refrigerant it uses.

The free AC refrigerant guide explains these changes in more depth for homeowners.

When an air conditioning repair stops making sense

Most repairs are worth doing. Age is the first thing to weigh: the Department of Energy's home cooling guide lists a life span of about 15 to 20 years for a central air conditioner. A failed capacitor on a 14 year old system is still a sensible repair. A failed compressor or a leaking evaporator coil on that same system, especially one that runs on R-22, is the moment to price both paths.

Ask for the repair quote and a replacement quote side by side, with the age of the equipment, the refrigerant type and the expected life of the repaired part noted. The repair or replace guide lays out the factors, and the AC repair cost guide covers what drives the price of common repairs.

Questions for the company doing the work

  1. Which component failed, and how did you confirm it? Ask for the reading or to see the part.
  2. What refrigerant does my system use, and does the repair involve it? If yes, ask about the leak search and how much refrigerant is being added.
  3. Is the replacement part matched to my equipment? Mismatched indoor and outdoor parts can hurt efficiency and humidity control.
  4. What warranty applies to the part and the labor? Get the terms in writing; they vary by company and manufacturer.
  5. What is your ACR license number? Then confirm it yourself on TDLR's website.
