Gas and carbon monoxide safety before any furnace repair

Natural gas burns inside the furnace, often just down the hall from the bedrooms. These two situations come before the cold, the repair and the bill.

You smell gas

CenterPoint Energy, the gas utility for most Houston homes, says to leave on foot immediately. Skip the light switches, phones, car and anything else that might spark, and leave the meter and valves alone. From a safe distance, call 911 and CenterPoint's gas leak hotline at 888-876-5786. Do not assume a neighbor already called it in.

The CO alarm goes off, or people feel sick

Carbon monoxide is a poison gas you cannot see or smell. According to the CDC, poisoning often starts with a headache, dizziness or weakness and can bring nausea, vomiting, chest pain or confusion; someone asleep can be overcome before waking. Move every person and pet outdoors, then dial 911.

  • Keep CO alarms working. Put them outside sleeping areas and on each floor, and test them when you test your smoke alarms.
  • Leave the burner area to a technician. Do not relight burners, jump a safety switch or take the burner door apart.
  • Keep fuel-burning equipment out of the house. No generators, grills or camp heaters indoors or in an attached garage, even with the door up.

Where Houston gas furnaces live, and why that matters

In many Houston homes the furnace is not in a basement. It sits on a platform in a hot attic, stacked under the air handler and coil that cool the house all summer, or it lives in a hall closet or garage. That location shapes repairs. Attic units bake through months of Gulf Coast heat while they wait, the filter and blower get a full cooling season of use, and wasps, rodents and dust find their way into burner compartments and vent pipes.

Across Texas, gas is actually the minority heat source. Citing Census figures, the U.S. Energy Information Administration put electricity as the main heating fuel in 61 percent of Texas homes, against roughly 35 percent for natural gas. If your system has no gas line, burners or flue pipe, it is likely a heat pump or electric strip heat, and our heating repair page for heat pumps and electric heat fits your situation better.

A gas furnace still depends on electricity. EIA points out that most modern gas furnaces need power to ignite and to run the fan that moves warm air through the ducts. So when the lights go out in a storm, the furnace goes quiet too, even with gas at the meter.

How a gas furnace starts, step by step

Most furnace problems are a break somewhere in a fixed start-up chain. Knowing the order helps you tell the assistant, and then the technician, exactly where your furnace stops. Typical modern furnaces follow a sequence like this:

  1. The thermostat calls for heat. A low-voltage signal tells the control board to begin.
  2. The inducer motor starts. This small fan pulls air through the burner area and pushes exhaust up the vent before any gas flows.
  3. The pressure switch proves the draft. If the vent is blocked or the inducer is weak, the switch stays open and the furnace stops here.
  4. The igniter heats up. On most newer furnaces a hot surface igniter glows bright orange. Older units may use a spark or a standing pilot.
  5. The gas valve opens and the burners light. Flame rolls across the burners within a few seconds.
  6. The flame sensor confirms the flame. If it cannot detect flame, the board closes the gas valve almost immediately. This is why a dirty sensor causes the "lights, then quits" pattern.
  7. The blower comes on. After the heat exchanger warms up, the main fan starts pushing warm air to the rooms.
  8. The limit switch keeps watch. If the furnace runs too hot, often because of a clogged filter or closed vents, the limit switch shuts the burners off.

What the February 2021 freeze taught Houston about furnace repair

Houston homeowners do not need furnaces often, but when they do, they really do. During the Valentine's week storm of 2021, Bush Intercontinental Airport dropped to 13 degrees on the morning of February 16, and the National Weather Service office for Houston and Galveston issued its first ever wind chill warning. NWS records show the City of Houston struggled to a high of only 25 degrees on February 15.

The grid failed at the same time. A University of Houston Hobby School report put the peak at close to 4.5 million Texas homes and businesses without power. A gas furnace with gas at the meter but no electricity cannot run its igniter or blower, so many Houston families with gas heat were cold anyway. Some tried to warm up with cars, grills or generators too close to the house, and the Texas Department of State Health Services later counted 19 storm deaths from carbon monoxide poisoning, from generators, grills, heaters and vehicles used in enclosed spaces and from ice blocking the vents of gas heating equipment.

  • Plan for an outage, not just a cold night. Extra blankets and a safe warm room beat any improvised heat source.
  • Generators go outdoors only, well away from windows, doors and vents, never in the garage.
  • After the power returns, watch the first few heat cycles. If the furnace will not light, short-cycles or smells wrong, call instead of resetting it repeatedly.

Fall checks before the first Houston cold front

A few minutes in October can save a cold call in December. These are safe for any homeowner:

  • Switch the thermostat to Heat on a cool afternoon and raise it a few degrees to confirm the furnace lights and warm air arrives.
  • Put in a fresh filter. ENERGY STAR suggests checking it every month; a summer of cooling leaves many Houston filters clogged.
  • Test the carbon monoxide alarms and replace batteries.
  • Clear the space around the furnace. Closet and garage furnaces need breathing room; boxes, paint cans and cleaning products do not belong there.
  • Look, do not touch, at the vent pipe where you can see it. A loose, rusted or disconnected flue is a reason to call.

For the inside of the furnace, ENERGY STAR advises a yearly checkup by a contractor covering gas connections, gas pressure, burner combustion and the heat exchanger, plus a check that the system starts, runs and shuts off properly. Our HVAC tune-up page explains what a full visit should include.

What a careful technician does on a furnace call

A good diagnosis follows the furnace through its start-up sequence and finds the exact step where it fails. On a no-heat call you can expect a technician to read any blinking error code on the control board, watch the inducer, pressure switch, igniter and flame sensor in order, measure gas pressure, and check temperature rise and airflow so the limit switch is not tripping. Because a furnace burns fuel inside the home, a thorough technician also looks at the flue, the burners and the heat exchanger, and tests for carbon monoxide around the appliance.
