AC installation in Houston, TX is a different job from installing the same equipment somewhere dry. The system has to pull water out of the air for most of the year, it usually sits partly in a hot attic, and in many neighborhoods the outdoor unit has to stay above floodwater. This page walks through a replacement in the order the decisions come up: whether to replace at all, how the size is chosen, what changed in new equipment, the City of Houston permit, the attic and the flood question, and a checklist to hold against every written quote.

Replace the system, or repair it one more time?

A unit that still cools is not automatically due for replacement because of its age. ENERGY STAR points to a group of signs that together make the case: equipment more than 10 years old, repairs that keep coming, power bills that creep up and rooms that never feel right. One sign by itself is a prompt to ask questions.

What failed matters most. A fan motor or a contactor is routine. A failed compressor or a leaking indoor coil can run close to the price of new equipment, and that is where the conversation usually turns.

Older refrigerant also tips the scale. Production and import of new R-22, the refrigerant in many systems installed before 2010, ended in the U.S. in 2020, so a leaking R-22 system gets harder to keep running.

Sizing for Houston humidity: Manual J, S and D

In a humid climate the right size is the one that matches the house, not the biggest unit that fits the pad. Three procedures from ACCA, the Air Conditioning Contractors of America trade group, are how a contractor gets there. Ask which ones the company ran.

  1. Manual J measures the load. It estimates how much heat and moisture your home picks up on a design day, from attic insulation, window size and orientation, wall type, air leaks and the number of people living there. It does not choose a unit.
  2. Manual S picks the equipment. It compares the Manual J numbers with manufacturer performance data, so the system can handle both the temperature load and the moisture load.
  3. Manual D sizes the ducts. A new unit cannot deliver its rated airflow through ducts that were too small or badly laid out to begin with.

Here is why oversizing hurts so much on the Gulf Coast. An air conditioner removes moisture when warm, damp air passes over a cold indoor coil and the water drips off into the drain. That takes running time. Building scientist Allison Bailes of Energy Vanguard notes that serious dehumidification starts only after about 15 minutes of run time. An oversized unit cools the thermostat quickly and shuts off before then, so the house reaches the set temperature but stays sticky. A National Renewable Energy Laboratory fact sheet notes that oversizing is very common because the ACCA methods often are not used. Matching the old tonnage, or sizing by square footage, is how it happens.

If the load falls between sizes, ask about two-stage or variable-speed equipment, which can run longer at lower output, and ask the company to show how its choice handles the moisture load.

What is different about new AC equipment in 2026

If your current system went in a decade or more ago, a new quote will use terms you may not have seen. Here is what they mean.

  • SEER2 minimums. Texas is in the Department of Energy's Southeast region. Since January 1, 2023, a new split central air conditioner installed here must be rated at least 14.3 SEER2 below 45,000 BTU per hour, or 13.8 SEER2 at 45,000 and above. Split heat pumps everywhere must meet 14.3 SEER2 and 7.5 HSPF2. In the Southeast the air conditioner rule applies by installation date.
  • A new class of refrigerant. Since 2025 the EPA's Technology Transitions rule has required new home systems to be built for refrigerants whose global warming potential is under 700. On a Houston quote that will usually read R-454B or R-32. Each carries an A2L safety class, which means mildly flammable, and many of these systems include a refrigerant detection system that starts the indoor blower to dilute a leak.
  • R-410A stock can still go in. A final EPA rule in spring 2026 dropped the deadline that would have stopped installers from putting in older R-410A equipment after January 1, 2026. Equipment built or brought into the country before 2025 may still be installed while supplies last. Servicing an R-410A system you already own was never restricted.
  • Matched pairs. The outdoor unit and the indoor coil have to be designed for the same refrigerant and rated together. The AHRI Directory lists certified combinations; ask for the AHRI reference number and you can look it up yourself.

The free AC guide explains the refrigerant change in more detail, including what it means if you only need a repair.

Houston HVAC permit for an AC replacement

Inside Houston city limits, replacing an air conditioner is permitted and inspected work. The Houston Permitting Center's page for condensing units says the City requires a permit to install, repair or replace one, that an inspection is required, and that plan review may be needed depending on the scope. Applications go through the City's iPermits system, and the fee is based on the value of the job. The permit is valid for 180 days.

A contractor cannot simply show up and file. The City requires anyone holding a state air conditioning contractor license to register that license with Houston Public Works before working inside the city, and the registration asks for a copy of the state license card and a certificate of insurance naming the licensee. Since January 2024, mechanical work in the City has been governed by the 2021 Uniform Mechanical Code with Houston amendments.

In practice the installing company pulls the permit. Check that the quote names who files it. Try to be there when the inspector comes, and file the passed inspection with your closing documents and warranty papers. Questions about a specific job can go to the City's Mechanical Section at 832-394-8850. If you live in Pearland, Sugar Land, League City, Katy or another suburb, or in unincorporated Harris or Fort Bend County, the rules come from a different office; ask the company which one applies.

Attic air handlers and the ducts behind the ceiling

In many Houston houses the indoor air handler, or the furnace and coil, sits in the attic. That space can get far hotter than the outdoors on a summer afternoon. Any leak in the ducts up there either dumps cooled air into the insulation or pulls hot, damp attic air into the system. ENERGY STAR puts typical duct losses from leaks, holes and loose connections at roughly a fifth to almost a third (20 to 30 percent) of the air the system moves. No efficiency rating on a new condenser can win that air back.

Ask what the quote does about the ducts: sealing, repairs, replacement of crushed sections, or a leakage test. Because the coil sweats all summer, also ask how the condensate is handled in the attic. A drain pan under the unit, a clear drain line and a safety switch that stops the system before an overflow reaches the ceiling are reasonable things to see listed.

Raising the outdoor unit in flood-prone areas

Floodwater ruins outdoor condensers. FEMA's guidance on protecting building utilities from flood damage describes raising HVAC equipment above the design flood elevation on platforms or pedestals, or moving it to a higher location, rather than leaving it at grade. The design flood elevation is the level your community adopts for flood protection, at least the base flood elevation and sometimes higher.

If your street has flooded before, or your home is in a mapped floodplain, ask the company whether the new unit should sit on a raised stand, how the stand will be anchored against wind, and whether the local floodplain office has a height requirement. Raising the unit is usually far cheaper during a replacement than after a flood.

What a careful installation includes

Ask the company to describe its process. These steps are where a careful job and a rushed one part ways.

  1. Recovering the old refrigerant. It goes into a recovery tank, never into the air. EPA rules under the Clean Air Act forbid venting it, and only an EPA certified technician may handle it.
  2. Placing the units. The condenser sits level, on a pad or a raised stand, with open space around it for airflow and for future service. Indoors, the coil or air handler gets a drain and protection against overflow.
  3. The copper lines. New lines are run, or the old ones are confirmed suitable for the new refrigerant. Joints are brazed and the system is pressure tested.
  4. Evacuation. A vacuum pump removes air and moisture from the lines, and the reading is held before refrigerant is released.
  5. Charge and airflow. Refrigerant is adjusted following the equipment maker's instructions. Fan speed is set to the airflow from the design, because in Houston that setting changes how much water the coil pulls out.
  6. Walking you through it. You learn the thermostat, get the warranty registration details and a date for the city inspection.

What AC installation costs, and what changes the price

National cost guides give a rough frame. Fixr's guide to central air installation puts the typical range at about $5,800 to $14,400, with homes that need new ductwork costing more. Treat that as a starting point only: it is a national figure, it is not a Houston price and it is not a price from this line or any company it connects you with. Real quotes vary with the size and efficiency of the system, how much of the indoor coil, copper and ductwork gets replaced, how hard the attic is to reach, whether the electrical service needs work, a raised stand and the permit. The AC repair cost guide covers the repair side of the same question.

Rebates and the federal tax credit in 2026

The federal Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit, often called 25C, covered qualifying equipment through 2025. The 2025 federal budget law ended it for anything placed in service after the last day of 2025. A system installed in 2026 earns no 25C credit, so be wary of a pitch that still subtracts one from the price.

CenterPoint Energy, which delivers electricity across much of the Houston area, has run efficiency programs that pay incentives on qualifying replacement air conditioners and heat pumps through participating distributors and contractors, for homes in its Houston electric service area. Programs change from year to year and funds can run out, so ask the company before the equipment is chosen whether it takes part, whether your system would qualify and who files the paperwork.

Questions to ask at the estimate

  • Will you do a Manual J on my house, and may I keep a copy?
  • Could this system be repaired instead, and at what price?
  • What is the AHRI reference number for this outdoor unit and coil?
  • How will this system handle humidity, not just temperature?
  • What will you do about the attic ducts and the condensate drain?
  • Should the outdoor unit be raised, given this lot's flood history?
  • Who pulls the permit, from which office, and who meets the inspector?
  • Is a CenterPoint incentive open right now, and does this equipment meet it?
