How to Size an Air Conditioner for Your Home: A Houston Homeowner's Guide

If you've ever walked into a home where the AC runs constantly but the house still feels muggy, or one where the system blasts cold air for five minutes and shuts off — you've experienced what happens when an air conditioner is the wrong size. In Houston's brutal heat and humidity, getting your AC size right isn't just about comfort. It's about energy bills, equipment lifespan, and whether your home actually feels livable from June through September.

This guide walks you through exactly how to size an air conditioner for your home, what factors matter most in the Houston climate, and when it's time to call in a professional to make sure you get it right the first time.

Why AC Sizing Matters More Than You Think

Many homeowners assume bigger is always better when it comes to air conditioners. It's not. An oversized AC unit will cool your home too quickly without running long enough to remove humidity from the air. In Houston — where relative humidity regularly sits above 70% — that's a serious problem. You'll end up with a cold, clammy house and a system that short-cycles, wearing itself out years before it should.

On the flip side, an undersized unit will run nonstop trying to keep up with the heat load, driving up your electricity bill and still failing to keep your home comfortable on the hottest days.

Proper AC sizing means your system runs in longer, efficient cycles that cool the air and dehumidify it — exactly what Houston homes need.

Understanding BTUs and Tonnage

Air conditioner capacity is measured in BTUs (British Thermal Units) per hour, or more commonly in "tons." One ton of cooling capacity equals 12,000 BTUs per hour. Residential systems typically range from 1.5 tons to 5 tons.

As a very rough starting point, the industry often uses a baseline of about 20 BTUs per square foot of living space. So a 2,000 square foot home might need around 40,000 BTUs — or roughly a 3.5-ton unit. But in Houston, that baseline almost always needs to be adjusted upward because of our extreme heat index and humidity levels.

Here's a general sizing reference chart to get you thinking:

  • 1,000 – 1,200 sq ft: 1.5 – 2 tons
  • 1,200 – 1,800 sq ft: 2 – 2.5 tons
  • 1,800 – 2,500 sq ft: 3 – 3.5 tons
  • 2,500 – 3,300 sq ft: 4 – 4.5 tons
  • 3,300+ sq ft: 5 tons or multiple systems

Remember: these are starting points, not final answers. Your actual needs depend on a lot more than just square footage.

Key Factors That Affect AC Sizing in Houston

A proper AC sizing calculation — called a Manual J load calculation — takes dozens of variables into account. Here are the most important ones for Houston homeowners:

1. Ceiling Height

Homes with vaulted or high ceilings have more air volume to cool. A 2,000 sq ft home with 10-foot ceilings needs significantly more cooling capacity than the same footprint with standard 8-foot ceilings.

2. Insulation Quality

Older Houston homes — especially those built before 1990 — often have inadequate attic insulation. Poor insulation means your home absorbs far more heat from the roof and walls, dramatically increasing your cooling load.

3. Window Size, Type, and Orientation

Large south- and west-facing windows let in enormous amounts of solar heat during Houston afternoons. Single-pane windows are particularly problematic. Low-E double-pane windows can meaningfully reduce your cooling load.

4. Home Age and Air Sealing

Drafty older homes lose conditioned air and let in hot, humid outside air. Cracks around doors, windows, and penetrations all add to the load your AC must handle.

5. Number of Occupants and Heat-Generating Appliances

People generate heat. So do ovens, computers, lighting, and other appliances. A home with six people and a busy kitchen generates more heat than an empty house of the same size.

6. Local Climate Data

Houston's climate is its own beast. We're in ASHRAE Climate Zone 2A — hot and humid — which means our cooling loads are among the highest in the country. Any sizing calculation must use Houston-specific design temperatures, which typically account for outdoor temps around 95°F with high humidity.

What Is a Manual J Load Calculation?

A Manual J calculation is the ACCA (Air Conditioning Contractors of America) industry standard for determining the right AC size for a home. It's a detailed engineering process that inputs all the factors mentioned above — square footage, insulation, windows, climate data, occupancy, and more — to produce an accurate cooling load number.

Reputable HVAC contractors in Houston will perform a Manual J before recommending a system. If a contractor simply looks at your home for two minutes and says "you need a 4-ton unit" without taking any measurements or asking detailed questions, that's a red flag. Guesswork leads to the oversizing and undersizing problems we talked about earlier.

Ask any contractor you're evaluating whether they perform Manual J calculations. The good ones will say yes without hesitation.

Signs Your Current AC Is the Wrong Size

Not sure if your existing system is properly sized? Here are telltale signs it might not be:

  • Short cycling: The system turns on and off frequently, running for only a few minutes at a time
  • High humidity indoors: The air feels sticky even when the temperature is at your setpoint
  • Uneven temperatures: Some rooms are cold while others stay warm
  • System runs constantly: The AC never shuts off even on mild days
  • Skyrocketing energy bills: Unexplained increases in your electricity costs during cooling season

Any of these issues warrant a professional evaluation — not just a new thermostat or refrigerant top-off.

When to Replace vs. Resize

If you're replacing an old system, don't automatically assume the new one should be the same size. Building codes, insulation standards, and window technologies have all changed. Your home may have had improvements made since the original system was installed. A new Manual J calculation will tell you whether your replacement system should be the same size, smaller, or larger than what you're removing.

Also consider variable-speed and two-stage systems, which are increasingly popular in Houston. These units can ramp up or down based on demand, handling both mild spring days and peak summer heat more efficiently than single-stage equipment — and they do a far better job at dehumidification.
